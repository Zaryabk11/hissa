## Layout

## Backend architecture rules (enforced by `npm run lint`)

1. Dependency direction: routes -> controller -> service -> repository. Never skip or reverse a layer.
2. A module imports another module only through that module's `index.ts`. Never its repository or internals.
3. External APIs (Gemini) sit behind clients inside their module, injected into services so tests can mock them.
4. Controllers hold no business logic. Repositories hold no business rules. Services never touch HTTP.
5. The backend uses the Supabase service role, which bypasses RLS: scope every query to the authenticated user.

## Product rules

- Gemini has three bounded jobs. In scan it only names dishes from our catalog (enum plus "unknown"). In Food and Feel it only words one approved question from a compact aggregate summary of the user's own check-ins; the service owns the intent, the options and their order, and falls back to a fixed question on a timeout, invalid JSON, a wrong option list or wording that fails an allowlist of words. Gemini never produces calorie numbers, counts, food restrictions, diagnoses or health claims. The third job is offline and one-off: `backend/scripts/draft-recipes.ts` drafts recipes for catalog dishes, guarded by a banned-word check; it never runs at request time, and nothing is served until a human sets `reviewed: true`.
- Every number shown to a user is labelled an estimate. Never give medical advice.
- A friend sees only username, display name, "logged today", the streak, nudge state and a goal the user chose to share, never meals, photos, calories or feelings.
- Notifications are inbox-only (read through `GET /api/notifications`); there is no push.
- Clients never write `meals`, `meal_items`, `feel_checkins` or friend, goal and notification data to Supabase directly; everything goes through the API.

:# Web Design & Conversion Principles
Follow these principles when designing or improving websites:

### 1. Visual Hierarchy

- Do not blindly follow the outdated F-pattern reading assumption. Users navigate websites in different ways.
- Establish a clear visual hierarchy: most important elements should be the largest and most prominent.
- Reduce visual emphasis on secondary information.
- Make important content immediately noticeable without forcing a particular reading path.

### 2. Buttons & CTAs

- CTAs must have strong contrast against surrounding elements.
- Make primary actions immediately identifiable.
- Avoid ghost buttons (outline-only buttons). Prefer solid backgrounds that make buttons visually prominent.
- Users should never have to search for the next action.

### 3. Colour Palette

- Use a consistent colour palette aligned with the brand identity.
- Prioritise accessibility. Maintain sufficient contrast between text, backgrounds, and interactive elements.
- Follow the **60-30-10 rule** as a starting point:

- 60% dominant colours (backgrounds, neutrals, whites, blacks, greys).
- 30% brand colours.
- 10% accent colours for CTAs and important elements.
- Validate colour contrast using WCAG contrast guidelines.

### 4. Typography

- Use typography to establish hierarchy and improve readability.
- **H1:** Largest and most prominent heading. Communicates the page's primary purpose.
- **H2:** Organises content into sections and guides scanning.
- **Body text:** Prioritise readability. Avoid decorative or overly stylised fonts for paragraphs.
- Keep typography consistent across the website.

### 5. Conversion-Focused Design

- Do not optimise for aesthetics alone. Every design decision should support the website's business objectives.
- Reduce friction and make it easy for users to complete desired actions.
- Prioritise three principles:

- **Clarity:** Immediately communicate who the business is, what it offers, why it matters, and what the visitor should do next.
- **Scannability:** Structure content so visitors can quickly scan, understand, and make decisions. Use clear headings, concise copy, and logical sections.
- **Motivation:** Address the visitor's actual needs, pain points, and reasons for taking action.
- Make purchasing, booking, signing up, or contacting the business as straightforward as possible.

### 6. Design for the Target Audience

- Design for the website's actual visitors, not the developer's or client's personal preferences.
- Research the target audience before choosing layouts, typography, colours, imagery, and messaging.
- Every design choice should serve the audience's expectations and needs.

### 7. Continuous Improvement

- Follow current web design and UX practices rather than relying on outdated conventions.
- Continuously evaluate and improve usability, accessibility, and conversion performance.
- Treat design decisions as hypotheses to validate, not personal preferences.

### Core Principle
**A beautiful website that fails to convert is a failed website.** Prioritise clarity, usability, accessibility, and business outcomes alongside visual quality.
